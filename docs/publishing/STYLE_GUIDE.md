# Puckworks editorial style guide

Puckworks answers espresso questions in ordinary language without relaxing the evidence underneath. The public voice is a technically serious coffee enthusiast with access to a simulation laboratory: curious, conversational, precise, candid about failed explanations, and lightly dry where natural.

## Opening and answer

Open with a coffee situation or question that works for someone who has never heard of Puckworks. Within roughly the first 120 words, establish what is familiar and why it matters. Give the narrow answer or current verdict within roughly the first 250 words. Do not begin with project history, repository design, evidence governance, definitions not yet needed, or a barricade of caveats.

## Voice and explanation

Use Tim Brewer's first-person singular voice when personal reasoning or work is relevant, and use “you” naturally. Do not impersonate an academic paper or fabricate Tim's experiences, equipment, brewing habits, experiments, or opinions. Avoid corporate-launch language.

Define a technical term when it becomes necessary. Prefer “Water is still finding a connected path through the puck” to “Hydraulic connectivity has not yet reached its terminal state.” Use analogies only when they preserve the relevant physics. Use at most one equation and preferably none.

Give the answer before the caveats unless immediate qualification prevents a false claim. Public limitations should be the few conditions that could materially change the answer, not a prose copy of the internal uncertainty inventory.

## Titles and reader value

Titles promise a recognizable question, useful answer, or surprising result. Useful forms include:

- What happens before the first drop of espresso?
- Why grinding finer can sometimes make espresso run faster
- Why nine bar is not a strength setting
- What actually controls how much caffeine reaches your cup?
- Should dark roast be ground finer or coarser?

These are editorial examples, not authorized scientific conclusions. Avoid vague project announcements, repository or governance terminology, manufactured controversy, sensational certainty, and titles that conceal the reader value.

## Backstage mechanics and practical implications

Do not place internal IDs, full commit identifiers, repository commands, validation output, or claim tables in public copy. A compact source note at the end is enough for most readers.

Evidence-supported practical actions are welcome when they are measurable, conditional, and useful as a diagnostic or experiment. State or imply the relevant conditions. Never recast a physical or chemical result as a taste optimum. Do not provide affiliate or sponsored recommendations, product rankings or endorsements, purchasing advice, objectively best recipes, unsupported taste or flavor predictions, universal grinder settings, or universal pressure or shot-time prescriptions.

Critique evidence and mechanisms, not people. Do not use: revolutionize, revolutionary, game-changing, journey, unlock the secret, shocking, proves once and for all, science says, everyone knows, objectively best, perfect shot, bad barista, delve, tapestry, groundbreaking, or “robust” without a defined statistical or engineering meaning. Do not say “the model shows.”

## Human cold-read test

- Would a coffee reader understand why to care after the first paragraph?
- Is there an answer before the article becomes technical?
- Does the title's promise get fulfilled?
- Is the article useful without knowing what Puckworks is?
- Is there one memorable idea?
- Is there one bounded action, diagnostic, or observation the reader can use?
- Are the limitations concise and material?
- Is any paragraph present only to satisfy internal process?
- Does the prose sound like Tim rather than a compliance template?
- Would removing the evidence machinery leave the scientific meaning intact?
